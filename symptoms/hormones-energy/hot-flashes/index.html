Hot flashes: why they happen and what helps

A hot flash is a sudden wave of heat across your face, neck and chest that can leave you flushed, sweaty and then chilled. When it happens at night it is called a night sweat, and it is often what wakes you at 4 am. Hot flashes are common, and they are treatable.

The short answer

  • Most hot flashes come from changing estrogen levels in perimenopause and menopause.
  • Each one usually lasts one to five minutes. For many women they come and go for several years.
  • Alcohol, caffeine, spicy food, stress and a warm bedroom can make them worse.
  • Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment. Non-hormonal prescriptions and lifestyle changes help too.

What a hot flash feels like

Most women describe a rush of heat that starts in the chest or face and spreads upward. Your skin may turn red, you may sweat, and your heart can beat faster. As it fades you may feel chilled, especially if your clothes or sheets got damp.

About three in four women have hot flashes during the menopause transition. Some have a few a week, others several an hour. How often they come and how much they bother you is what guides treatment.

Common causes

Falling and fluctuating estrogen affects the part of the brain that controls body temperature, so it reacts to small changes as if you were overheating. That is why the most common causes are hormonal:

  • Perimenopause
  • Menopause
  • Premature menopause
  • Overactive thyroid

Hot flashes can also come from some medicines, including certain breast cancer treatments and antidepressants, from surgery that removes the ovaries, and less often from infections or other conditions. That is why new hot flashes are worth mentioning to a provider, especially if they don't fit your age or stage of life.

Triggers worth tracking

Triggers don't cause hot flashes, but they can set one off or make it stronger. A simple note on your phone for two weeks often shows a pattern:

  • Alcohol, especially in the evening
  • Caffeine and hot drinks
  • Spicy food
  • Smoking
  • Stress and anxiety
  • A warm room, heavy bedding or tight layers

What helps

You do not have to wait them out. Most women find relief with one of these, or a mix:

Hormone therapy

Estrogen, often with progesterone, is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats. It suits many healthy women under 60, and not everyone.

Non-hormonal options

Several prescription medicines reduce hot flashes for women who can't or prefer not to use hormones.

Cooler nights

A cool bedroom, breathable layers, less evening alcohol and a steady sleep routine can take the edge off.

When to see a provider

Book a visit if any of these sound familiar:

  • Hot flashes or night sweats disrupt your sleep, work or relationships
  • They started before age 40
  • They came back, or started, years after menopause
  • They come with fever, unexplained weight loss or a racing heart at rest
  • You want to talk through hormone or non-hormonal treatment

Hot flashes FAQ

How long do hot flashes last?

A single hot flash usually lasts one to five minutes. For many women they continue on and off for several years around menopause, and for some they last longer.

Why are hot flashes worse at night?

Hot flashes during sleep are called night sweats. Warm bedding, a warm room and alcohol in the evening can all make them more likely, and waking up drenched makes them feel worse than daytime flashes.

Can I have hot flashes before menopause?

Yes. Many women first notice them in perimenopause, while they are still having periods. Hot flashes can also come from other causes, such as an overactive thyroid or some medicines.

Do I need blood tests for hot flashes?

Not always. In your 40s and 50s, your age and symptoms usually tell the story. Your provider may check thyroid levels or other blood work if something does not fit the usual pattern.

Night sweats in women: why they happen and what helps

You wake up at 3 or 4 in the morning, hot, damp and wide awake. You throw off the covers, then lie there feeling cold and wondering why this keeps happening. If that sounds familiar, you are not alone, and it is not something you simply have to put up with.

The short answer

  • Night sweats are hot flashes that happen while you sleep.1
  • In your 40s and 50s they are very often part of perimenopause and menopause. Up to 80% of women have hot flashes or night sweats during the transition.3
  • They can last for years. In a large US study, frequent symptoms lasted a median of 7.4 years.3
  • Some medicines, an overactive thyroid, sleep apnea and infections can cause them too, so new night sweats are worth checking.2
  • Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment, and there are proven non-hormonal options.4,5

What night sweats feel like

A night sweat usually starts as a sudden wave of heat in your chest, neck or face. Your heart may race, and you may sweat enough to soak your pajamas or sheets. As it fades you can feel chilled, and then it is hard to fall back asleep. For many women the lost sleep is the hardest part, because it shows up the next day as tiredness, low mood and trouble focusing.

Waking up warm because the room is hot or you have too many blankets is different. That kind of sweating is uncomfortable but usually not a sign that anything is wrong.2

Why they happen in perimenopause and menopause

In the years around your last period, estrogen levels rise and fall unevenly before settling lower. These changes affect the part of your brain that controls body temperature. It starts to react to small changes as if you were overheating, so blood rushes to your skin and you sweat to cool down. When that happens at night, it wakes you up.1,6

Night sweats often start in perimenopause, while you are still having periods. They also tend to last longer than women expect. In SWAN, a US study that followed more than 3,000 women through midlife, frequent hot flashes and night sweats lasted a median of 7.4 years and continued for about 4.5 years after the last period. Women whose symptoms started earlier tended to have them for longer.3

Other causes worth ruling out

Not every night sweat is hormonal. Other common causes include:2

  • Some medicines, including antidepressants and diabetes medicines that lower blood sugar
  • An overactive thyroid
  • Anxiety
  • Sleep apnea and other sleep disorders
  • Alcohol use
  • Infections, such as tuberculosis
  • Less often, some cancers, such as lymphoma

That is why a good visit looks at your sleep, your medicines and your overall health, not only your hormones.

Triggers that can make nights worse

Triggers do not cause night sweats, but they can set one off or make it stronger. Writing down what happened on the nights you wake up, for two weeks or so, often shows a pattern:1

  • Alcohol, especially in the evening
  • Caffeine
  • Spicy food
  • Smoking
  • A warm bedroom, heavy bedding or warm nightclothes

A cooler room, layered bedding, a fan and a glass of cold water by the bed can make nights more comfortable.1 Studies have not shown that cooling tricks or avoiding triggers reduce how often hot flashes happen, though.5 If night sweats still wake you, it is worth talking about treatment.

Treatments that work

You do not have to wait them out. These are the options with the best evidence behind them:

Hormone therapy

The most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats. For healthy women under 60, or within 10 years of their last period, the benefits generally outweigh the risks.4 It is not right for everyone, for example some women with a history of breast cancer, blood clots or stroke.

Is HRT safe?

Non-hormonal prescriptions

The Menopause Society recommends certain antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs), gabapentin, oxybutynin and fezolinetant.5 A newer medicine, elinzanetant, was approved by the FDA in 2025.6

Options without medicine

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and clinical hypnosis both have good evidence for making hot flashes less bothersome, and losing weight can help some women.5

Sleep better

The right choice depends on how much night sweats affect your life, your health history and what matters to you, which is why it is made with a provider who knows your history.

When to see a provider

Book a visit if your night sweats:2,7

  • Happen regularly or keep interrupting your sleep
  • Come with a fever, weight loss you can't explain, a cough, diarrhea or pain in one area
  • Started months or years after your other menopause symptoms ended
  • Started before age 40

Night sweats FAQ

Are night sweats a sign of menopause?

They are one of the most common signs of perimenopause and menopause, and they often start while you are still having periods. They can also have other causes, such as some medicines, an overactive thyroid or an infection, so it is worth checking with a provider.

Why do I wake up hot at night?

In your 40s and 50s, a common reason is the hormone change of perimenopause, which affects the part of the brain that controls body temperature. A warm room, heavy bedding or alcohol in the evening can also do it. If it keeps happening, talk to a provider.

How long do night sweats last?

For many women they come and go for several years. In the SWAN study, frequent hot flashes and night sweats lasted a median of 7.4 years. Treatment can reduce them in the meantime.

Can I have night sweats while I'm still having periods?

Yes. Night sweats often begin in perimenopause, while periods are still coming but changing.

Do I need blood tests to find out if it's perimenopause?

Often not. For women aged 45 and over, perimenopause is usually identified from new hot flashes or night sweats plus changes in your periods, without lab tests. Your provider may test your thyroid or other levels if something does not fit the usual pattern, or if you are under 45.

Is hormone therapy safe for night sweats?

For healthy women under 60, or within 10 years of their last period, the benefits of hormone therapy for night sweats generally outweigh the risks. It is not right for everyone, so your provider will look at your personal and family history first.

Can I talk to a provider without coming in?

Yes. Your first 15-minute consult is a video call, so you can start from home.
